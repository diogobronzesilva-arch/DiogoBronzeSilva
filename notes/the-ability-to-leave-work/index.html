The ability to leave work

A job should matter enough to do well, and not so much that it follows you into every room.

There is a version of ambition that is easy to recognise because it is always visible. It answers late. It is permanently available. It has opinions about optimisation, routines and performance, and it treats every empty hour as capacity waiting to be used.

I understand the appeal. Work rewards attention. Careers often advance because someone cared more, prepared better, stayed longer or took responsibility when it would have been easier not to.

But there is another skill that ambition rarely advertises: the ability to leave work.

Not abandon it. Not become careless. Leave it.

There is a difference.

For a long time, professional seriousness has been associated with availability. Technology made that association easier to indulge. Work used to have physical edges. You left the office and, unless something was genuinely urgent, the work stayed there. Now the office lives in a pocket. A message can enter dinner without knocking.

The danger is not only exhaustion. It is proportion.

A job that follows you into every room eventually becomes the room.

I do not want work to be small in my life. I spend too many hours doing it for that. I want it to be useful, demanding and done well. I want to improve at it. I want to earn well enough to support a large family. I want the satisfaction of being trusted with difficult things and becoming capable of harder ones.

But precisely because work matters, it needs a boundary. Anything good can become distorted when it is asked to become ultimate.

Family is useful here because it is gloriously uninterested in professional abstraction. A child does not care that the quarter is important. Dinner is still dinner. Someone still needs help. A story still has to be read. The person in front of you cannot be put in a backlog because your forecast changed.

That can feel like interruption if work is treated as the main reality and everything else as the gap between working sessions. I think the order should be reversed. Work belongs inside a life. The life does not belong inside the spaces work leaves unused.

This is not an argument for doing less by default. Sometimes good work requires an intense week, a late evening, a difficult month. Responsibility includes knowing when exceptional effort is actually required.

But exceptional effort only remains exceptional if there is a normal state to return to.

The ability to close the laptop is partly practical and partly moral. It says that attention has other legitimate claims on it. A spouse. Children. Friends. Prayer. A book. A garden. A body that needs rest. Silence.

Ambition becomes much easier to live with when it has a proper place. You can care deeply about work without asking work to explain who you are. You can pursue excellence without making every other good compete with it on professional terms.

Do the work well. Be reliable. Care more than is fashionable. Then, when the day is over, be capable of being somewhere else entirely.
