The work that holds the rest

The modern world invites us to mistake convenience for independence. We only ever build on what others quietly sustain.

Yesterday, during a car ride with my eldest son, I realised he had never stopped to think about what happens before a fresh loaf of bread reaches our kitchen table.

It was an ordinary moment, but it opened up something larger. In our home, mornings begin naturally. With five children, we rarely need an alarm; we wake early, sit down together, and share breakfast without frantic school runs or manufactured urgency. That quiet ritual—one of the anchors of our family life—feels effortless. Yet every element of it depends on labour carried out in the dark, hours before we open our eyes: flour measured, dough kneaded, ovens lit, vans loaded and driven across cold motorways. A moment that cements our family bonds begins in the hands of people we will likely never meet.

The modern world is designed to make this labour invisible. We live inside systems engineered to minimise friction, where goods simply arrive, services appear on demand, and comfort is treated not as a quiet privilege, but as an entitlement. In doing so, we risk confusing convenience with self-sufficiency.

This blindness does not stop at the kitchen table; it follows us directly into professional life.

In business and modern careers, prestige gravitates towards the abstract and the visible: the strategist, the dealmaker, the presenter of dashboards, the architect of broad visions. Yet no enterprise survives on strategy alone. Every company, every institution, and every economy stands on a foundation of people doing essential, unglamorous work—processing invoices, answering support queries, maintaining servers, driving freight, and cleaning the desks where high-level decisions are made.

When organizations forget this, they develop a dangerous form of corporate myopia. They begin to confuse the steering wheel with the engine. They treat operational and manual labour as mere cost lines to be squeezed rather than as the very bedrock of their existence.

True professional maturity begins with the recognition that nobody builds anything alone. Respect cannot be a veneer reserved for clients, executives, or people whose approval we seek. Real character in leadership is revealed by how one treats the individuals who hold no immediate power over our careers—those whose contribution is so consistent that it is simply taken for granted until it stops.

The conversation with my son started with bread, but it was really about how to stand in the world. It was about teaching him that dignity belongs to all honest work, and that the highest form of respect is awareness: the willingness to pause, look beyond the final result, and acknowledge the human effort that made it possible.

Before a leader can command, or an organisation can grow, or a family can enjoy the peace of an early morning, someone had to do the work. The least we can offer them is our gratitude, our humility, and our deepest respect.
