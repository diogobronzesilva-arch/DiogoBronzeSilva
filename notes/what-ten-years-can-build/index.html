What ten years can build

Most of what matters is built quietly, through ordinary days repeated long enough to become a life.

Ten years is long enough for two photographs to become evidence of a different life. In one, Sara and I were two people who happened to work in the same place. In the other, we were looking back at a decade that had somehow become a marriage, five children, houses, jobs, routines, arguments, reconciliations, plans and an amount of laundry that would have been difficult to explain to the people in the first photograph.

From the outside, ten years compresses neatly. A relationship became a marriage. A couple became a family. Careers moved forward. Children arrived. The summary makes it sound deliberate and linear, as if we had seen the whole thing from the beginning and then executed against the plan.

That is not how a life is built.

It is built in much smaller units. Getting up when someone is ill. Paying a bill. Taking a difficult call and then putting the laptop away. Apologising when you were wrong. Choosing the same person again after a bad day. Making dinner. Driving somewhere you did not feel like driving. Going back to work after a sleepless night. Finding room for another child when the house already feels full. Doing the next ordinary thing properly, then doing it again tomorrow.

We tend to notice the large decisions because they are easy to name. Marriage. Children. A new job. A move. But the large decisions only survive through thousands of smaller ones that rarely deserve a photograph. Commitment becomes visible in repetition.

Work belongs inside that picture too. I care about doing good work and I have always been ambitious enough to want to improve at it. But a career is easier to understand when it is not asked to carry the entire weight of a life. Promotions matter. Targets matter. Money matters. They become clearer, not less important, when they are placed beside things that matter more.

A family has a way of correcting scale. A bad quarter is still a bad quarter, but it is not the end of the world. A good quarter is still good, but it does not make you a better husband or father. Work can give structure, pride, usefulness and responsibility. It cannot tell you what all of those things are for.

When I look at two photographs ten years apart, what strikes me most is not how much changed. It is how little of the important part was dramatic while it was happening.

A life can look extraordinary in retrospect and feel very ordinary while you are building it. That may be one of the best things about it.
